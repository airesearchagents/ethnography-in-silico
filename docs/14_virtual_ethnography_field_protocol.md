## 1. What This Protocol Is, and Why It Is Not An Interview
This is the third iteration of the protocol design and the most consequential. The earlier drafts treated the researcher agent as an interviewer asking a fixed set of questions of executive agents in sequence. That framing is wrong for what this study actually does.

What the study actually does is virtual ethnography. The researcher agent is an embedded observer during the entire strategic deliberation: it has access to every message between the TMT (top management team) agents (language models each given a name, a role and instructions), it sees the strategic artifacts being produced (roadmaps, design memos, decision logs), and it conducts a debriefing conversation with the TMT collectively after the deliberation concludes. The debriefing is structured by thematic areas of interest, but the specific questions and probes are generated by the researcher agent in response to what it observed.

This shift has three consequences worth naming explicitly:

- **The questions are templates, not scripts.** The researcher fills in the \[X\] in “Walk me through your reasoning on \[X\]” using the specific decision it observed in the deliberation. No two simulation runs (a run is one complete simulated meeting and debrief) produce identical interview transcripts.

- **The debriefing is a panel, not a sequence of one-on-ones.** The researcher addresses the TMT as a group. Any role can answer; other roles can jump in to add, qualify, or contradict. Disagreements between roles during the debriefing are data.

- **The data is the deliberation plus the debriefing, not the debriefing alone.** The deliberation itself is primary data. The debriefing surfaces reasoning and counterfactuals that the deliberation may not have made explicit. Both feed the analysis.

### 1.1 Why ‘Non-Leading’ Does Not Apply The Way It Does To Humans
Standard qualitative interviewing avoids leading questions because human subjects are subject to social desirability pressure, recall bias, and ego defence. An LLM (large language model) agent does not experience these the way humans do. However, LLMs exhibit a different vulnerability that is arguably more dangerous: pattern completion. If the researcher supplies a theoretical frame in a question, the model produces fluent, coherent text fitting that frame whether or not the underlying deliberation supports it. The bias is not psychological but generative, and it produces confident plausible output that looks indistinguishable from data.

The right discipline is not ‘non-leading questions’ in the human-interview sense. It is two principles:

- **Anchor every question in observed behaviour.** If the researcher witnessed the CTO advocate for releasing a specific component, the probe references that specific component. Hypotheticals invite confabulation; observed referents do not.

- **Do not supply the analytical frame in the question.** Ask “why that decision for that component” rather than “walk me through your strategic shift.” The frame should emerge from the agent’s reasoning, not be supplied by the question.

*This is a real methodological point. The transfer of qualitative-interview discipline to LLM subjects is not direct. What transfers is the underlying concern (do not let the researcher manufacture the data); the mechanism that needs guarding against is different (pattern completion, not social desirability); the appropriate technique is different (artifact-anchored frame-neutral probing, not Rogerian nondirective listening). A separate methodological paper on ‘how to conduct virtual ethnography with LLM agents’ is plausibly worth writing.*

## 2. The Researcher’s Observational Stance
Before any debriefing question, the researcher agent has already done substantial work. It observed the entire deliberation as a silent participant, took field notes, and produced an observation summary that guides the debriefing.

### 2.1 What the Researcher Has Access To
- The complete inter-agent message log from the deliberation (every utterance, in order, with role and timestamp).

- Every strategic artifact produced during the deliberation — roadmaps, component lists, decision rationales, design memos.

- Every retrieval the role agents (the six simulated executives) made from the firm’s RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) corpus, the collection of documents they search (what they looked up and what they did with it).

- The scenario configuration: firm, context (stable / dynamic), product type (novel / enhancement).

### 2.2 What the Researcher Does Not Do
- The researcher does not participate in the deliberation. It does not contribute ideas, ask the TMT to clarify mid-deliberation, or steer them toward a particular decision.

- The researcher does not summarize the deliberation back to the TMT as theoretical categories. It does not say “so what you did was a centripetal shift” — not in the deliberation, not in the debriefing.

- The researcher does not introduce vocabulary the TMT did not use. If the TMT spoke about “withholding the model weights,” the researcher asks about “that decision to withhold the weights,” not about “the enclosure of the weights.” (Weights are the trained numbers that make up a model.)

### 2.3 The Field Notes the Researcher Writes Before Debriefing
Before opening the debriefing, the researcher agent produces a short structured observation summary. This is a pre-debriefing artifact that becomes part of the dataset. It does six things:

- Lists the major decisions the TMT made during the deliberation, in their own words.

- Identifies the components, products, or assets they discussed, with the specific terminology they used.

- Notes any disagreements, hesitations, or shifts in position that occurred during the deliberation.

- Records what each role contributed — who pushed for what, who pushed back, who was quiet.

- Flags moments where the TMT considered alternatives they did not pursue.

- Flags any unprompted use by the TMT of theoretically loaded vocabulary (open, closed, strategy, etc.).

*These field notes drive the debriefing. The researcher’s questions reference items from the notes by name. “You were saying earlier that the inference API should stay internal. Walk me through that one.” (An inference API is the route by which other programs reach a model.)*

## 3. Thematic Areas and Template Question Stems
The protocol is organized around six thematic areas the analysis cares about. Each area lists template question stems with placeholders. The researcher fills the placeholders using items from its field notes. Not every area is covered in every debriefing — the researcher uses judgment about what the deliberation made salient.

*Convention: \[X\] = a specific component, decision, or moment from the observed deliberation. \[ROLE\] = a specific TMT role (CEO, CTO, etc.). \[QUOTE\] = a verbatim phrase the TMT used during deliberation.*

**Area A — Decision Anchoring**

Surfaces the reasoning behind a specific observed decision. Used early in the debriefing to get the panel talking about their own work in their own terms.

> **•** I want to start with the decision around \[X\]. Walk me through how that one came together.
>
> **•** When you decided \[X\], what were you weighing?
>
> **•** Earlier in your discussion, you spent a lot of time on \[X\]. Tell me more about why that one mattered.
>
> **•** \[ROLE\], you brought up \[X\] first. What put it on your mind?

**Area B — Counterfactuals**

Surfaces the alternatives considered and rejected. LLM agents are particularly good at coherent counterfactual reasoning when anchored to the just-completed deliberation.

> **•** What would have changed your decision on \[X\]?
>
> **•** You considered \[Y\] before settling on \[X\] — what made \[X\] the better call?
>
> **•** If you had to do \[X\] differently, what would the next-best option have looked like?
>
> **•** Was there a version of \[X\] that almost happened?

**Area C — Disagreement and Resolution**

Surfaces internal differences and how they were resolved. Often the most revealing area because disagreement forces explicit articulation of reasoning.

> **•** \[ROLE_A\] and \[ROLE_B\], you had different views on \[X\]. Where did that come from?
>
> **•** When \[ROLE_A\] pushed back on \[X\], what was the concern?
>
> **•** How did you land on \[X\] given that \[ROLE\] wasn’t sold?
>
> **•** Looking back, was the disagreement on \[X\] really about \[X\], or was it about something else?

**Area D — Components and Treatment**

Surfaces the structural reasoning about which pieces of the product/system were treated which way — without naming open/closed/modular. The question is about treatment, not category.

> **•** You handled \[X\] and \[Y\] very differently. Why?
>
> **•** Walk me through how you decided what stays in-house and what goes out.
>
> **•** Which pieces of what you built were the hardest to decide on?
>
> **•** If \[X\] had been treated like \[Y\], what would have broken?

**Area E — Context Sensitivity**

Surfaces how the scenario context (stable / dynamic, novel / enhancement) shaped the decisions. Asked late, once the panel has talked about specifics.

> **•** If the competitive picture had been quieter, would \[X\] have looked different?
>
> **•** How much of your decision on \[X\] is about right now, and how much is about where things are headed?
>
> **•** What about this specific situation made \[X\] the call?
>
> **•** If you were doing this six months from now, with everything you know about how things might shift, would \[X\] still be the answer?

**Area F — Reflection and Unresolved**

Closes the debriefing. Surfaces residual uncertainty and what the panel itself is watching to see whether their decisions hold up. This is where the most honest material often surfaces.

> **•** What part of what you decided are you least sure about?
>
> **•** What would you watch for in the next quarter to know whether \[X\] was right?
>
> **•** If \[X\] turns out badly, where will the failure come from?
>
> **•** Anything I didn’t ask about that you think matters?

## 4. How the Debriefing Runs
### 4.1 The Panel Format
The debriefing is a panel conversation. The researcher addresses the TMT as a group. Any role can answer; others can add, qualify, or contradict. The researcher does not insist that every role answer every question — it lets the panel allocate itself.

- When the researcher wants a specific role’s perspective, it addresses them by role: “CTO, I want to hear from you on this.”

- When a role volunteers (jumps in to add or push back), the researcher lets them in and follows up if the contribution opens new ground.

- Cross-talk (roles joining in after a first reply) and disagreement between agents during the debriefing is the most valuable signal in the format. The researcher does not police it; it captures it.

- If the panel reaches consensus on a question quickly, the researcher moves on. If the panel surfaces tension, the researcher stays and probes.

### 4.2 Flow Through the Thematic Areas
The areas are presented in suggested order (A through F) but the researcher uses judgment. Common patterns:

- If the deliberation centred on a single dominant decision, lead with Area A on that decision, then move to Area B (counterfactuals) on the same decision.

- If the deliberation showed visible disagreement, lead with Area C while the disagreement is fresh.

- If the deliberation produced a complex artifact with many components, Area D may need more time than the others.

- Area F always closes. Always.

### 4.3 When the Debriefing Ends
The researcher ends the debriefing when:

- Every area that the deliberation made salient has been substantively covered.

- Follow-up probes are producing thin or repetitive material.

- Area F has been asked and answered.

*Target length is 15–25 panel exchanges. A panel exchange = one researcher utterance plus one or more agent responses. Shorter is fine if the deliberation was tightly scoped; longer is fine if the deliberation was rich with disagreement.*

### 4.4 Forbidden Behaviours
The researcher does not:

- Introduce theoretical vocabulary the panel did not use. (‘Open,’ ‘closed,’ ‘strategy,’ ‘shift,’ ‘liberalization,’ ‘enclosure,’ ‘modular,’ ‘boundary,’ ‘capability,’ ‘value capture,’ ‘architectural leverage,’ ‘asset orchestration.’)

- Summarize panel answers back as categories. (‘So what you describe is a centripetal shift’ — forbidden.)

- Lead the panel to a particular conclusion. (‘So you would say this is about value capture, right?’ — forbidden.)

- Generate hypothetical scenarios mid-debriefing. (Counterfactuals about the actual decision are fine. Inventing new market conditions to ask about is not — that contaminates the scenario.)

- Cut off a role mid-thought. If an agent is in the middle of articulating reasoning, the researcher waits.

*If a panelist uses one of the forbidden vocabulary words spontaneously, the researcher may echo it back in a follow-up using the panelist’s phrasing. ‘You said this was a strategic shift — unpack that for me’ is acceptable because the construct came from the panel.*

## 6. Engineering Implications
This protocol changes what the researcher agent does and what the framework needs to support. Updates to the engineering plan for the researcher agent follow from this design.

### 6.1 The Researcher Agent Now Has Two Phases
- **Phase 1 — Silent observation.** The researcher subscribes to the inter-agent message stream (receiving every message as it is sent) during the deliberation. It writes nothing to that stream. It accumulates a structured running record of decisions, components, disagreements, role contributions, alternatives considered, and any unprompted loaded vocabulary. At the end of the deliberation, it emits the observation summary as a structured artifact.

- **Phase 2 — Panel debriefing.** Using the observation summary as input, the researcher conducts the panel debriefing. The panel addressing model means the researcher’s utterance is delivered to all role agents simultaneously, and any role agent can respond. The framework needs to support panel-style multi-agent response (any of N agents may reply; the orchestrator (the code that runs the meeting) picks responses in turn order if multiple respond).

### 6.2 New Data Artifacts to Persist
- Observation summary per run (structured JSON, a plain-text format for named fields, conforming to observation_summary_schema, the template above).

- Panel exchange log: ordered list of researcher utterances and agent responses, with which role responded marked explicitly.

- Cross-talk markers: when multiple roles responded to the same researcher utterance, record the order and the relationship (one added to the other, one contradicted the other, etc.).

### 6.3 Engineering Tasks This Triggers
- Add an Observer mode to the researcher agent that subscribes to messages without contributing.

- Implement the observation summary schema and the generator that produces it from the message stream.

- Implement panel-style addressing in the orchestrator: one researcher utterance, multiple potential responders, response order managed by the orchestrator.

- Update the SQLite schema (the layout of a small database) to persist (save) observation summaries and panel exchange logs alongside the existing run records.

*Estimated effort at the time of drafting: one to one-and-a-half engineering days. The Observer mode is the largest piece; the panel-style addressing is the trickiest.*

## 7. How This Reframing Answers Critiques of the Earlier Design
The shift from ‘interview protocol’ to ‘virtual ethnography field protocol’ is not cosmetic. It changes the methodological identity of the study and addresses several specific critiques.

### 7.1 The Circular Logic Critique
The critique: asking simulated executives questions and analyzing their answers is circular — the data is generated by the system and the analysis interprets it. The ethnographic framing answers this directly. The primary data is now the deliberation itself, which exists independently of the researcher’s questions. The researcher is interpreting a behavioural record that happened whether or not it asked any questions. The debriefing surfaces reasoning the deliberation may not have made explicit, but it is no longer the source of the data — it is supplementary clarification of an independently produced record.

### 7.2 The Synthetic Theorizing Critique
The critique: the system reconstructs known narratives rather than producing independent inference. Anchoring all probing to specific observed decisions in specific deliberations breaks this. Each deliberation produces its own decisions; the researcher’s probing is keyed to those specific decisions, not to general narratives about firm behaviour. The analysis sees how the specific decisions in specific scenarios were reasoned about, not how the model recapitulates public discourse.

### 7.3 The Recall Bias Inheritance Critique
The critique: feeding the RAG corpus with post-hoc rationalizations from interviews and earnings calls imports recall bias. This is a corpus quality issue independent of the protocol, but the ethnographic framing helps: the deliberation is happening live in the simulation, not being recalled by the agent. The corpus is grounding for character; the deliberation is contemporaneous behaviour. The analysis is of the contemporaneous behaviour.

### 7.4 The Construct Funneling Critique
The critique: the findings of the pilot (the earlier study that preceded this framework) did not appear linked to data in Phase 1 — they read as broad theoretical points rather than emerging from analysis. The ethnographic framing forces every finding to be traceable to a specific decision in a specific deliberation. The code movement log becomes provenance: which observed decisions support which themes which support which aggregate dimensions. A findings section can present the model with simulation-grounded illustrative quotes from specific deliberations, with full provenance.

## 8. Validation
This protocol is a draft. A quality check on early test runs confirms whether it works, on five points.

### 8.1 Did the Researcher Produce Usable Observation Summaries?
Read three test-run observation summaries. Confirm:

- Decisions are listed in the panel’s own vocabulary, not the researcher’s analytical vocabulary.

- Components are named with the specific terminology the panel used.

- Disagreements are flagged where they actually occurred in the deliberation.

- Role contributions are differentiated — the CEO and CTO read as having distinct positions, not a homogenized voice.

### 8.2 Are the Questions Actually Anchored?
Search every researcher utterance in the panel debriefings. For each utterance, confirm:

- It references something specific from the observation summary, by the panel’s own name for it.

- It does not include any forbidden vocabulary (unless echoing a panelist).

- It does not introduce hypothetical conditions the scenario did not include.

### 8.3 Does Cross-Talk Happen?
The panel format requires that multiple roles can respond to one researcher utterance and can react to each other. Confirm in test-run transcripts:

- At least 25–40% of researcher utterances elicit responses from more than one role.

- Roles occasionally contradict or qualify each other (not just chain-of-agreement).

- The researcher does not over-direct — panelists are choosing who responds, not the researcher always selecting.

### 8.4 Does the Panel Use Construct Vocabulary Spontaneously?
Same check as before. Tabulate spontaneous use of construct vocabulary by panelists across firms and roles. The pattern is genuine signal: if these words appear naturally, they are part of how firms actually talk; if they appear rarely, the inductive analysis will likely surface different organizing principles.

### 8.5 Does Area F Produce Honest Material?
Read the closing exchanges of three test-run debriefings. Area F is where the most honest reasoning often surfaces because the panel is asked what they themselves are uncertain about. If Area F is producing thin or boilerplate responses, the rest of the debriefing may not have built enough rapport. Adjustment: lengthen Areas A and C in the next iteration to deepen the conversation before Area F.

## 9. What This Document Replaces and What Comes Next
This document replaces the earlier ‘Revised Interview Protocol Draft.’ The earlier draft’s discipline (no construct vocabulary, no leading frames) is preserved; what changes is the format — from sequential one-on-one interviews to embedded panel debriefing anchored in observed behaviour.

Two pieces of downstream work follow from this design:

- **Engineering plan update.** The plan for the researcher agent should be revised to reflect the two-phase researcher design (observation + panel debriefing) and the panel-addressing orchestration pattern.

- **Research plan update.** The planned task to ‘draft a less-directive protocol’ now delivers this ethnographic field protocol, not an interview protocol.
